Economics in context · The richest and poorest in Asia
🌏
The coursebook reports that India’s richest 1% own 73% of the country’s wealth, while the poorest 50% own just 1%.
The coursebook asks you to discuss two questions about your own country. Jot your ideas about Thailand, then compare.
1 To what extent is there inequality of income and wealth in Thailand? 2 How could the government do more to reduce it?
1 · How unequal? The World Bank put Thailand’s income Gini at 43.3 in 2021 — the highest in the East Asia and Pacific region — and found that in 2019 the top 1% received about 21% of national income while the bottom half received about 14%. Gaps run between Bangkok and the regions (especially the Northeast and the South) and between formal and informal workers: just over half of Thai workers are informal, and many of them work in agriculture on much lower incomes. Wealth is more unequal still — land, shares and family businesses are concentrated in a small number of families, and Thailand lacks good data on wealth.
2 · What more could be done? Use the coursebook’s list: raise and enforce minimum wages (but they only reach formal workers); extend social insurance and transfers to informal workers; make income tax more progressive and widen the tax base; strengthen the inheritance tax and the Land and Buildings Tax; and improve the quality of state schools and health care outside Bangkok. The World Bank’s own advice focused on skills, education and regional development — the causes in coursebook section 14.3.

Watch the measure: Thailand’s Gini is about 43 when it is calculated from household income, but about 34 when it is calculated from household spending (World Bank, 2023). Both are “Thailand’s Gini”. Always say which one you are using.

Activity 2 · Gini Explorer

See what a Gini coefficient actually measures

The Gini coefficient runs from 0 (everyone has the same income) to 1 (one person has all of it). The coursebook’s Table 14.2 writes it as a percentage, so 0.45 and 45% mean the same thing. It comes from a Lorenz curve: the share of total income received by the poorest x% of the population. The further the curve sags below the diagonal, the bigger the orange area A, and Gini = A ÷ (A + B).

Part C · Think like an economist: inequality in Malaysia
🇲🇾
Consider whether it is better for Malaysia to have a more inclusive but slower rate of economic growth.
Reducing inequality has been a Malaysian policy aim for 50 years. The Gini fell from well over 0.5 in 1970 to 0.4 in 2018, and fewer than 1% of people are in absolute poverty — but the earnings gap between the top 20% and the rest has nearly doubled since 2000, pushing more people into relative poverty. Some economists argue that inequality is not a bad thing, because it gives people and firms an incentive to work, take risks and become more efficient. Plan a two-sided answer with a judgement.
For more inclusive growth. Extra income matters more to a poor household than to a rich one, so a more equal spread of the same income can raise total welfare. Coursebook section 14.3 says inequality is a barrier to growth: poor households cannot afford education, health care or credit, so human capital and enterprise are wasted. Large gaps also cause social and political tension, which deters investment.
For faster growth. The incentive argument: rewards for skill, effort and risk drive entrepreneurship and efficiency. Faster growth creates jobs and the tax revenue that pays for transfers and public services. With absolute poverty already below 1%, the priority might be raising everyone’s income rather than narrowing the gap.
It depends on. How inclusion is achieved — spending on education and health can raise both equality and long-run growth, whereas very high tax rates may slow growth; how big the growth sacrifice really is; and whether rising relative poverty is causing social problems.
Judgement. The choice is partly false: well-designed inclusion (better schools, health care and access to credit) supports growth over time, so Malaysia does not have to choose. It would be wrong to sacrifice much growth for equality only where redistribution relies on heavy taxes that damage incentives.

3(a) Explain the difference between a progressive direct tax and a regressive indirect tax. [8]
Plan before you write. There are two differences to explain: direct vs indirect (what is taxed and who pays the government) and progressive vs regressive (what happens to the percentage of income paid as income rises). Use numbers: Thailand’s income tax runs from 0% to 35%, and its VAT is 7%.
Your answer
Model answer. A direct tax is a tax on income or wealth, paid straight to the government by the person who bears it — for example personal income tax. An indirect tax is a tax on spending on goods and services, such as VAT; it is collected by sellers, who pass much of it on to buyers in higher prices.
A tax is progressive when the average rate — tax paid as a percentage of income — rises as income rises. Thailand’s personal income tax is progressive: the first ฿150,000 of net income is tax-free and the marginal rate then rises in steps from 5% to 35%. Someone with ฿300,000 of net income pays ฿7,500, an average rate of 2.5%; someone with ฿1,000,000 pays ฿115,000, an average rate of 11.5%. Because the rich pay a larger share of their income, the tax narrows the gap between post-tax incomes.
A tax is regressive when the average rate falls as income rises. VAT is charged at the same 7% on almost everything, but it is regressive in relation to income, because low-income households spend almost all of their income while high-income households save a larger share. A household earning ฿15,000 a month that spends it all pays about ฿980 in VAT — roughly 6.5% of its income. A household earning ฿150,000 that spends ฿80,000 pays about ฿5,230 — more baht, but only about 3.5% of its income.
So the two taxes differ in what they tax (income vs spending), in who hands the money to the government (the earner vs the seller), and in their effect on distribution: the progressive direct tax reduces inequality, the regressive indirect tax increases it.
Why this scores: all four terms are defined; “progressive” is defined by the average rate, avoiding the coursebook Tip’s classic mistake (“the rich pay more tax” is also true of a proportional tax); both taxes are illustrated with calculated numbers; the answer ends by linking each tax to inequality.

3(b) Discuss the ways in which a government can redistribute income in a lower middle-income country. [12]
Take note of the context: lower middle-income. Thailand is classified by the World Bank as upper middle-income, so use countries such as Pakistan or Bangladesh (the coursebook’s examples), or India. Analyse three or four policies, evaluate each one in that context, then make a judgement about which works best. Plan in note form first. The Redistribution Simulator is your evidence.
Your plan
Model plan.
0 · Context. In a lower middle-income country the informal economy is large, only a small share of people pay direct taxes, and tax evasion and corruption are common (coursebook 14.4). Every policy below is judged against that.
1 · Minimum wage. Raises the pay of low-paid formal workers, cutting poverty among them. But it does not reach the informal sector, the self-employed or family businesses — in South Africa about 47% of workers were paid below the new minimum in 2019 — and if demand for labour is elastic it may cause unemployment (Figure 14.3).
2 · Transfer payments. Pensions, child benefits and cash transfers move income to those who cannot work or earn least, and can be targeted. But they depend on tax revenue and on a reliable register of who is poor; in Pakistan, pensions cover only the formal sector. Targeted cash schemes that pay through ID cards or mobile phones, such as Pakistan’s Benazir Income Support Programme, are one answer. Benefits withdrawn as earnings rise may discourage work.
3 · Progressive income tax, inheritance and capital taxes. A rising average rate narrows post-tax incomes and funds the other policies. But the tax base is narrow, so revenue is small; high top rates encourage avoidance, evasion and emigration; inheritance and capital taxes have only a small overall impact.
4 · State provision. Free primary education and basic health care are worth the same to every family, so they are worth far more as a share of a poor family’s income. They also build human capital, tackling a cause of inequality (14.3). But they are expensive, and quality is often poor in rural areas.
5 · Judgement. In a lower middle-income country, state provision of education and health care, together with well-targeted cash transfers, is likely to be the most effective, because these reach informal workers whom the minimum wage and income tax miss. Progressive taxes matter mainly as a way to fund these policies — and that depends on widening the tax base and reducing corruption over time.
Why this scores: every policy is analysed and evaluated in the stated context; the evidence is specific (South Africa, Pakistan); the judgement says which policies work best and why in this kind of economy, rather than listing them all as equally good.
