Activity 1 · The choice behind the curve

Build for tomorrow, or spend today?

Every robot arm in a Rayong car plant, every new BTS carriage and every container crane at Laem Chabang uses land, labour and materials that could have made things Thai households enjoy right now — phones, motorbikes, clothes, a mookata dinner on Friday night.

In the decades before the 1997 crisis, Thailand put around 40% of GDP into investment — new capital goods — and the economy grew by more than 7% a year on average. In April 2026, Finance Minister Ekniti Nitithanprapas said investment is now only about 24% of GDP, and warned that it is not enough to keep up with a changing world. Growth in recent years has been closer to 2–3%.

More capital goods today means fewer consumer goods today. How much fewer? That is what you will measure on the curve.

Figures: Ekniti Nitithanprapas, reported by VNA, 13 April 2026; pre-1997 growth from Ammar Siamwalla, Out of the Frying Pan: The Thai Economy Between Two Crises.

Activity 2 · Coursebook 5.1

What every point on the diagram means

A production possibility curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its current resources and its current state of technology, when all those resources are fully employed. It is sometimes called the production possibility frontier, because it draws a boundary between what can be produced and what cannot.

The diagram below is the coursebook's economy: it makes only cars and televisions. Four positions are marked. Work out what each one tells you before you look at the answer.

Figure 1: The production possibility curve for cars and televisions. Points A and B lie on the curve, C lies inside it, D lies outside it.
On the curve
Efficient. Every resource is employed and used in its best combination — the economy is getting all it can from what it has.
Inside the curve
Inefficient. Output is less than the available resources could produce. Possible, but wasteful.
Outside the curve
Unattainable. Not possible with current resources and technology — this is scarcity.
✓ All five done. The single most common Paper 2 error on this topic is describing a point inside the curve as "impossible". It is entirely possible — it is just wasteful. Only points outside are impossible.

Notice the axes. A PPC always has a good on each axis — never price, never quantity of one good against time. If you label one axis "price" you have drawn a demand diagram by mistake, and no marks follow.

Activity 4 · Coursebook 5.3

Why is the curve bowed outwards?

A PPC can be drawn as a straight line or as a curve that bows outwards from the origin. The shape is not decoration — it tells you whether opportunity cost stays the same or rises as production is reallocated, and that difference is a standard four-mark question.

Constant opportunity cost
A straight line. The gradient is the same everywhere, so each extra unit of Good A always costs the same amount of Good B.
Increasing opportunity cost
Bowed outwards. The curve gets steeper as you move right, so each extra unit of Good A costs more Good B than the one before.

On the curve, every move gave Thailand the same gain — 100m units of consumer goods — but the cost kept rising: 30m, then 50m, then 70m units of capital goods. This is called opportunity cost. It is the reason the PPC is to the origin (bowed outwards) rather than a line.

Why does the cost rise? Factors of production are not equally to making both goods. At first, Thailand switches the resources that are productive in capital goods — general workers and simple tools that can make household fans just as easily as factory parts. Later, it has to switch resources that are highly in capital goods, such as the robot welding lines and trained engineers at car-parts plants in the Eastern Economic Corridor. Moved into consumer goods, they add very little output, while a large amount of capital goods output is . There is a second reason too: as more and more workers crowd into consumer goods factories with a fixed number of machines, each extra worker adds less output than the one before. This is returns.

If every resource were equally good at making both goods, each 100m units of consumer goods would cost the same amount of capital goods. Opportunity cost would be , and the PPC would be a straight line — like the dashed line you added in the Drag the Curve tab.

The choice also has a cost in the . Capital goods are used to make other goods. If Thailand chooses point D rather than point A, it produces fewer capital goods, so its stock of capital grows more and its PPC shifts by less in the years ahead. This is why the Finance Minister is worried that investment has fallen from about 40% of GDP before 1997 to about 24% today.

The underlying question in every case is the same: are the economy's resources equally good at producing both goods, or not?

The reason in one sentence

Factors of production are not perfectly substitutable between uses. When Thailand shifts resources out of rice and into electronics, the first workers and land to move are the ones least suited to farming and most suited to assembly — so little rice is lost for a lot of electronics. Keep going and you are eventually moving skilled paddy farmers and prime delta land into circuit boards, giving up a great deal of rice for very little extra output.

That rising sacrifice is exactly what a curve bowing outwards draws. A straight line would say a rice farmer and an electronics technician are interchangeable — true for two similar styles of trainers made in the same factory, and almost never true across whole industries.

Your answer
Model answer — 4 marks — data → definition → chain → trade-off

At point D, Thailand produces 400m units of consumer goods but only 90m units of capital goods, compared with 240m at point A. Capital goods, such as machinery and infrastructure, are used to produce other goods, so producing fewer of them means less investment. As existing machines wear out, the capital stock grows slowly or may even fall. Because productive capacity depends on the quantity and quality of capital, Thailand's PPC will shift outwards by less in future. However, households gain more consumer goods and higher living standards now — the opportunity cost is lower potential output later.

Why this scores well: it quotes the diagram (400 / 90 / 240), defines capital goods in the answer, links fewer capital goods → less investment → slower growth of capacity → smaller outward shift, and names the trade-off in the last sentence. It also keeps the two ideas apart: choosing D is a movement along today's curve; the smaller outward shift happens later.

Does the whole curve move — or not at all?

A PPC shifts only when productive capacity changes: the total ability of the economy to produce. That happens for two reasons — more or better resources become available, or technology changes. Anything else moves you along the curve, or towards it, but leaves the curve exactly where it was.

The choice that decides how far the curve shifts next year

Put capital goods on one axis and consumer goods on the other and the PPC shows the hardest choice a lower-income economy faces. Producing almost entirely consumer goods leaves so little for capital goods that new investment barely replaces the machinery wearing out — so the curve does not move, and next year the same choice returns.

Give up a relatively small amount of consumer goods and the gain in capital goods can be proportionally much larger, because the curve is steep at that end. That extra capital raises productive capacity, shifting the whole curve outwards. The cost is paid now, by people consuming less today; the benefit arrives years later. That is why the decision is genuinely difficult rather than obvious.
