Activity 1 · Key terms

Output = income = expenditure

Coursebook Chapter 15 is the first macroeconomics chapter. National income is a country’s total output. People earn an income from producing that output, and the income is then spent on it. So there are three ways to measure the same thing — and economists add the words gross or net, domestic or national, and market prices or basic prices to say exactly what they measured.

Economics in context · Nigeria’s output grew 90% overnight
🇳🇬
On 5 April 2013, Nigeria’s statisticians changed the way they measured output. Overnight, the figure rose by about 90%.
They started counting industries that had been left out or underestimated, such as the Nollywood film industry and telecommunications. Average income appeared to rise from $1,500 to $2,688, and Nigeria seemed to overtake South Africa as Africa’s largest economy. In 2018 Nigeria’s output was $398 bn and South Africa’s $368 bn — but average income was about three times higher in South Africa.
1 Why do governments measure their country’s output? 2 Were Nigerians richer on 5 April 2013 than on 4 April 2013? 3 How can total output be higher in Nigeria but average income three times higher in South Africa?
1 · Why measure output? To judge how well the economy is performing (is output growing at a steady, sustainable rate?), to compare with last year and with other countries, and to decide policy — if output is growing more slowly than the economy is capable of, the government may act to stimulate it. International organisations such as the United Nations and the World Bank use the figures to compare countries.
2 · Richer overnight? No. Nothing more was produced on 5 April than on 4 April: the measurement changed, not the output. Nigerians’ real living standards were the same. The lesson: national income figures are estimates, and a change in method can move them a long way.
3 · Total vs average. Average income = total output ÷ population. Nigeria had far more people — about 196 million in 2018 against South Africa’s 58 million — so its larger total is shared between many more people: about $2,000 each, against about $6,300 in South Africa. (The coursebook prints Nigeria’s population as 96m; the 2018 figure was nearer 196m, which is what makes South Africa’s average about three times higher.)

Remember the coursebook Tip: the words in the name of a measure tell you what it includes. “Gross” = total (including the investment that only replaces worn-out capital), “domestic” = produced inside the country, “national” = earned by the country’s residents, “product” = output.

Activity 2 · Size and growth

How big? How fast?

Economists look at two things: the level of a country’s output (how big is it?) and its rate of growth (how fast is it getting bigger?). A huge economy can add more dollars of output in a year while growing more slowly in percentage terms, because it starts from a larger base. Part C · Is Thailand ASEAN’s 2nd or 3rd largest economy?
🇹🇭
By the IMF’s estimates of GDP for 2025, Singapore’s economy was slightly larger than Thailand’s, so Thailand came 3rd in ASEAN. By the World Bank’s GNI figures for 2025, Thailand came 2nd, behind Indonesia.
How can both be true? Think about what GDP measures and what GNI measures. Hint: many foreign multinational companies have factories, banks and regional offices in Singapore.
GDP measures output produced inside a country, whoever owns the firms. GNI measures the income of the country’s residents, wherever it is earned. Singapore hosts a very large number of foreign multinationals; their output counts in Singapore’s GDP, but the profits they send home to their owners abroad are taken away to get GNI. Historically Singapore’s GNI has been lower than its GDP. So on a GDP ranking Singapore can be ahead of Thailand, and on a GNI ranking behind it. The ranking depends on the measure — always say which one you are using. You will meet this again in Activity 3.
Activity 3 · GDP or GNI?

Made here, or earned by us?

GDP is the value of output produced by factors of production based in a country, whoever owns them. GNI changes the focus to the income earned by the country’s residents and firms, wherever they earn it:

GNI = GDP + net income from abroad — mainly net property income (profits, interest and dividends) plus net compensation of employees (wages of residents working abroad for short periods, such as seasonal and cross-border workers).

Activity 4 · Three methods

Three ways to measure GDP — one answer

The output method adds up the value added by every industry. The income method adds up the wages, rent, interest and profit earned producing that output. The expenditure method adds up spending on it: C + I + G + (X − M), including changes in stocks. Each should give the same total, because they measure the same circular flow. Part A shows the trap in the output method; Part B lets you prove the three methods agree.

Activity 5 · Measure converter

Market or basic? Gross or net? Domestic or national?

Every measure is a step on the same ladder. Market prices → basic prices: take away taxes on products (indirect taxes) and add subsidies. Domestic → national: add net income from abroad. Gross → net: take away depreciation (capital consumption), the capital goods worn out or out of date this year. Examiners report that fewer than 40% of candidates got a question like Part C right in November 2024 — usually because of the order or the sign of a step.

Part B · Think like an economist: Vietnam and Laos
🇻🇳
“National income data indicated that economic activity was greater in Vietnam than in Laos in 2019.”
1 What national income data would you need to check this statement, and where would you find it? 2 Apart from comparing economic activity between countries, how else can national income data be used?
1 · Data. GDP for both countries in the same year and the same currency (usually US$); GNI as well, because foreign-owned firms and workers abroad can make GDP and GNI differ; population, so you can compare output and income per head — the statement also claims people in Vietnam had a higher average income; and ideally figures adjusted for prices (real, or purchasing power parity), because the same dollar buys more in a lower-cost country. Sources: the World Bank’s World Development Indicators, the IMF, each country’s statistics office, the CIA World Factbook, The Economist Pocket World in Figures.
2 · Other uses. To measure growth over time (this year vs last year, or 50 years ago — the Key Concept “Time”); to guide policy — if output is growing more slowly than it could, the government or the Bank of Thailand may act to stimulate it; to help firms plan investment; to classify countries — the World Bank groups countries by GNI per head, and the UN’s Human Development Index uses GNI per head; and to judge living standards — with care, because the figures leave out the informal economy and say nothing about how income is shared.
Part C · Write it up: an 8-mark explain answer
8
Explain what is meant by gross domestic product (GDP) and gross national income (GNI), and why a country’s GNI may be higher or lower than its GDP. [8]
Paper 2 style. Define both terms precisely, show the link between them, then explain the flows that make them differ — in both directions — with an example of each.
Your answer
Model answer. Gross domestic product is the total value of output produced by the factors of production based in a country in a year, whoever owns them. “Gross” means total, “domestic” means inside the home economy and “product” means output. So Thailand’s GDP includes the cars made in Rayong by Japanese-owned firms.
Gross national income changes the focus from where output is produced to who earns the income. It is the total income earned by a country’s residents and firms, wherever they earn it. GNI = GDP + net income from abroad.
Net income from abroad is mainly net property income — profits, interest and dividends received from abroad minus those paid to foreigners — plus net compensation of employees, the wages of residents working abroad for short periods, such as seasonal workers.
GNI is higher than GDP when residents earn more from abroad than foreigners earn in the country. Japan’s multinationals own factories all over Asia and their profits flow home, so Japan’s GNI was about 4% above its GDP in 2018; the Philippines’ was 20% higher, mainly because many Filipinos work abroad.
GNI is lower than GDP when foreign multinationals and foreign workers contribute a large part of output, so more income flows out than in. Qatar depends on foreign workers; Ireland and Thailand have attracted a lot of foreign investment, and the profits paid to the foreign owners make GNI smaller than GDP.
Why this scores: both terms are defined precisely, the link between them is stated as a formula, the components of net income from abroad are named, and both directions are explained with a real example each.
