Activity 4 · The decision that earns the marks

Shift or movement? Which curve? Which way?

Twelve scenarios, two steps each. Step 1: does the cause shift a curve, or move you along one? Step 2: name the curve and the direction. Each correct step scores 1, so 24 points are available.

The rule underneath every answer: only a change in the product’s own price moves you along a curve. Everything else shifts one.

In an exam, write the decision down explicitly: “This is a shift of the supply curve to the left, not a movement along it, because the cause is a rise in costs and not a change in the price of the good itself.”

8
Explain, with the help of a diagram, how a sharp rise in the price of petrol is likely to affect the market for cars. [8]
Plan before you write: which curve, which way, why, and what happens to price and quantity. A labelled diagram with D, S, axes and both equilibria earns marks of its own.
Your answer
Model answer. Petrol and cars are complements: they are in joint demand, so they are consumed together. A sharp rise in the price of petrol raises the cost of running a car, which makes consumers less willing and less able to buy cars at every price. The demand curve for cars therefore shifts to the left, from D to D₁, because the cause is not the price of cars themselves. At the original price there is now excess supply, so price falls. As price falls there is a contraction of supply, a movement along the supply curve, until a new equilibrium is reached at a lower price and a lower quantity. Fewer resources are then allocated to car production.
Why this scores: it names the relationship (complements), explains the behaviour, identifies the shift and its direction, distinguishes the shift from the movement along supply, and finishes at the new equilibrium. Five linked steps, not five separate facts.

12
Discuss whether a change in income is always the most important influence on the demand for a product. [12]
A 12-mark discuss question needs two-sided analysis and a supported judgement. Plan it here in note form — four or five bullet points is enough — then compare with the model plan.
Your plan
Model plan.
1 · Case for income. For normal goods, higher income raises willingness and ability to pay, shifting demand right. Income effects are large for cars, foreign holidays and restaurant meals. Effective demand requires ability to pay, so income is always part of the story.
2 · Case against. For inferior goods such as instant noodles, rising income shifts demand left, so the direction is not even fixed. Substitutes can matter more: a fall in the price of tablets can cut laptop demand sharply. Fashion and taste can outweigh both, and price itself still determines the quantity demanded along the curve.
3 · It depends on. The type of good (normal, inferior, luxury, necessity); the size of the income change relative to price changes; the time period; how close the substitutes are.
4 · Judgement. Income is usually the most important influence for goods with a high income elasticity of demand, such as luxury cars, but for necessities with close substitutes the price of those substitutes matters more. The honest conclusion is that it depends on the good — supported by an example, not asserted.
Evaluation sentence starters: “This depends on whether…”, “In the short run… but in the long run…”, “This argument assumes… which may not hold because…”, “On balance, the stronger argument is… because…”
