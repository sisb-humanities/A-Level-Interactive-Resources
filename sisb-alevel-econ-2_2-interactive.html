Activity 1 · Key terms

Match each term to its meaning

Topic 2.1 asked which way quantity moves. Topic 2.2 asks how much. Three measures do the work: PED (own price), YED (income) and XED (the price of another good). All three are the same idea — a percentage change divided by a percentage change — so get the twelve terms below secure and the maths looks after itself.

Work through activities 1–8 after the lessons, then activity 9 as a timed test. Every calculation, table and figure on this page comes from coursebook Chapter 8 (pages 66–79), so your notes and the screen should agree.

Activity 4 · PED and total revenue

Two products, one price rise, opposite results

This is coursebook Figure 8.9. Both products currently sell at $10 and both sell 100 units a day, so both take $1000 a day. De is price elastic (PED = 2 at this point) and Di is price inelastic (PED = 0.5). Drag the price and watch total revenue move in opposite directions.

Activity 7 · Decisions that turn on an elasticity

Eight real decisions

Section 8.5 of the coursebook asks what firms and governments actually do with these numbers. Each card below is a decision. Choose, then read why — the reasoning matters far more than the click.

Notice how often the answer is “it depends on the elasticity value”. That sentence, with a number attached, is what turns description into evaluation.

All elasticity values are estimates drawn from past data, and past data may have been affected by things other than price. Saying so, once, with an example, is a genuine evaluation point — the coursebook makes it on page 76.

Step 2 · The 8-mark answer
8
Explain how knowledge of the price elasticity of demand for its product could help a firm decide whether to raise its price. [8]
Plan before you write: define PED, state the two cases, explain the effect on total revenue in each, and use a numerical example. A labelled diagram with an elastic and an inelastic curve earns marks of its own.
Your answer
Model answer. Price elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the product’s own price, and is calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Its value tells the firm what will happen to total revenue, which is price multiplied by quantity. If demand is price inelastic, for example PED = −0.5, then a 10% rise in price causes only a 5% fall in quantity demanded, so revenue rises: a product selling 100 units at $10 takes $1000, but 95 units at $11 takes $1045. Raising price is therefore sensible. If demand is price elastic, for example PED = −2, the same 10% rise in price cuts quantity demanded by 20%, so revenue falls from $1000 to $880 and the firm should not raise price; cutting price would raise revenue instead. The firm should also check the value it is using, since PED changes along the demand curve and is only an estimate based on past data.
Why this scores: it defines PED, gives the formula, splits into the two cases, attaches a number to each, follows each through to total revenue, and adds one limitation. That is knowledge plus a complete chain of analysis, not a list of facts.

Step 3 · The 12-mark answer
12
Discuss whether knowledge of income elasticity of demand is more useful to a business than knowledge of price elasticity of demand. [12]
A 12-mark discuss question needs both sides and a supported judgement. Plan it here in note form — four or five bullet points is enough — then compare with the model plan.
Your plan
Model plan.
1 · The case for YED. YED forecasts demand years ahead, which is what investment decisions need. A firm making luxury goods with YED = +2.5 knows that a 5% fall in incomes cuts its sales by 12.5%, so it can plan capacity and stock before the recession arrives. YED also guides the product mix: holding some goods with negative YED alongside superior goods protects the firm in a downturn, which is the point of Activity 8.6.
2 · The case for PED. PED is about a decision the firm controls directly and can take this week — its own price. It converts straight into total revenue, which YED cannot do, and it also tells the firm how exposed it is to a price cut by a rival once XED is taken into account. Income is outside the firm’s control; price is not.
3 · It depends on. The type of good — YED matters far more for luxury cars than for rice; the time horizon — PED for this quarter’s pricing, YED for a five-year investment; the stability of the economy; and the reliability of the estimates, which for YED need data over a long period and so are the less reliable of the two.
4 · Judgement. Neither is “more useful” in general: they answer different questions. For a short-run pricing decision PED is more useful because it links directly to revenue; for a long-run investment decision in an economy with rapidly rising incomes, such as Thailand or Vietnam, YED matters more. A firm that used only one of them would misjudge either its pricing or its capacity.
Evaluation sentence starters: “This depends on whether…”, “In the short run… but in the long run…”, “This estimate assumes… which may not hold because…”, “On balance, the stronger argument is… because…”
