Activity 1 · Coursebook 2.3

Positive or normative?

A positive statement is based on facts or actual evidence and can, in principle, be tested against the real world. A normative statement contains a value judgement — it says what ought to happen — and cannot be proven right or wrong.

The trap that costs the most marks: a positive statement does not have to be correct. "Raising the minimum wage will increase youth unemployment" is positive even if the evidence turns out to disagree, because evidence could settle it. Drag each statement into the right bin.

Quick test: could two economists with the same data settle this by looking harder at the evidence? If yes it is positive. If they would still disagree because they value different things, it is normative.

Activity 2 · Coursebook 2.1

Microeconomics or macroeconomics?

Microeconomics is the study of individual markets — the behaviour and decisions of households and firms, and how they interact. Macroeconomics is the study of the whole economy, or a group of economies, usually with some form of government involvement.

Sort each Thai example. Then read the tip — because the boundary is genuinely blurred, and knowing why is itself an AS-level point.

The division is not clean, and the coursebook says so explicitly: a rise in Thai households' demand for imported cars is a micro decision that feeds straight into the macro trade balance. Ask which level the question is set at, not which topic the word belongs to.

Activity 3 · Coursebook Activity 2.1, extended

Turn a normative statement into a positive one

This is the single most useful drill in the whole topic, because it forces you to strip out the value judgement and leave behind a claim that evidence could actually test. Type your version, then reveal a model answer and compare.

You are not aiming for identical wording. You are checking three things: has the "should / best / unfair / too much" gone? Is there a measurable variable? Could someone in principle collect data to check it?

✓ All five revealed. Notice what every model answer does: it names a variable, a direction of change and — where possible — a size or a timeframe. That is the skeleton of a positive statement.
Activity 4 · Coursebook 2.5

Short run, long run, or very long run?

Time periods in economics are defined by what can be varied, not by a number of months. There is no rule that says the short run is a year.

Short run
At least one factor input can be changed, but not all. Typically labour varies while capital is fixed.
Long run
All factors of production are variable, but the state of technology is constant.
Very long run
All key inputs vary — including technology, regulation and social attitudes.

Exam tip: whenever a question says "in the short run" it is telling you something is fixed. Say what is fixed in your answer — that sentence alone often unlocks the analysis mark.

Activity 5 · Paper 1 style

Exam-style multiple choice

Methodology questions appear early in Paper 1 and are free marks if your definitions are sharp. Two of these are the coursebook's own; the rest are in the same style.

✓ All six attempted. Methodology also shows up inside data-response questions — Paper 2 Section A often asks you to identify a normative statement in the source material and explain why it is one. "Because it contains a value judgement and cannot be tested against evidence" is the answer that scores.

Why the examiner cares about methodology

Every evaluation mark you will ever earn depends on this topic. Recognising that a claim rests on an assumption, that a model is a simplification, or that a statement is a value judgement dressed up as a fact — that is AO3.

So when you write "assuming ceteris paribus…" or "this depends on the time period considered…", you are not padding. You are doing exactly what the mark scheme rewards.
