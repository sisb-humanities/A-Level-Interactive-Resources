Activity 1 · Key terms

Match each term to its meaning

Ten terms carry this whole topic. Two pairs are easy to confuse: free good and public good, and public good and merit good. Read the meanings carefully before you drag.

Do activities 1–5 after the lessons, then activity 6 as a timed test. Missed the Soi Light Game in class? Play activity 4 first.

Drag these meanings
Onto the matching term

“Free” in economics means zero opportunity cost. It does not mean “provided free by the government”. A state school is free to use, but it is not a free good.

Activity 2 · The two tests

Excludable? Rival? Build the classification grid

Every good can be tested on two characteristics. Excludable: can people who don’t pay be stopped from using it? Rival: does one person’s use reduce what is left for others?

Answer both steps for each of the twelve goods. Each correct step scores 1. The grid at the bottom fills in as you go, and every good is placed in its correct box, whatever you answered.

The classification grid
Green = both steps right · orange = one right · red = neither.

The two quasi-public boxes are each missing a different characteristic of a public good. Free goods such as sunlight and air sit outside this grid altogether, because they are not scarce.

Activity 3 · Five types of good

Free, private, public, merit or demerit?

Drag each good into its type. Two per type. The hardest ones are the goods that governments provide free of charge: ask whether they are non-excludable and non-rival, or whether they are provided free for a different reason.

Into the right type
✓ All ten correct. Look at the merit goods bin: both goods are provided free by governments, but they are still rival and excludable, so they are not public goods. The free goods bin holds things that are not scarce, not things that are free to use.
Activity 4 · The free-rider problem

Will anyone pay for the street light?

Play five rounds against eleven neighbours. Watch what happens to payments as the rounds go on, and what changes when the soi committee installs a gate in round 5. Then explain what you saw.

Activity 5 · Analysis chains

Build the chain: from information failure to market failure

Merit and demerit goods are market failures because of information failure. Click the six steps of each chain in causal order. Start with what consumers don’t know, and finish with what happens to resources.

✓ Both chains complete. The two chains are mirror images: underestimated harm → over-consumption → too many resources; underestimated benefit → under-consumption → too few resources. Every merit or demerit good answer should name information failure and reach the misallocation of resources.
