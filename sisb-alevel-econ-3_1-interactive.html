Market failure does not mean the market has collapsed. It means the price mechanism has produced an inefficient allocation of resources — too much of one thing, too little (or none) of another.

8
With the help of a diagram, explain why healthcare may be under-provided by the free market and how a government could correct this. [8]
Plan before you write. Define a merit good, give the two reasons it is under-consumed (information failure and ability to pay), use Figure 12.2 to show OX and OY, then explain the government’s two options. Activity 3, Part A is the model to copy.
Your answer
Model answer. Healthcare is a merit good: a good that is more beneficial to consumers than they realise. In a free market it is under-consumed for two reasons. First, there is information failure: people underestimate the benefits of check-ups, vaccinations and early treatment, so their demand is lower than it would be with full information. Second, private providers charge a price, so access is restricted to those who can afford it. On the diagram, the private market settles where demand meets supply, at quantity OX and price P. The government judges that OY is needed to meet the needs of the population, shown by the vertical supply line Sgovt. The gap OY − OX is under-provision: too few resources are allocated to healthcare, which is a market failure. The government could correct this in two ways. It could take over provision entirely and supply OY itself, free at the point of use and funded from taxation. Alternatively, it could let private hospitals continue to supply OX to those who can pay P, and provide only the difference, OY − OX, for those who cannot afford private fees — the approach behind Thailand’s Universal Coverage Scheme alongside private hospitals. The second option is cheaper, which matters because every baht spent on healthcare has an opportunity cost.
Why this scores: it defines the term, gives two distinct causes rather than one, labels the diagram in the text (OX, OY, P, Sgovt), names the gap as a misallocation of resources, and explains both corrections with a real example.

12
Discuss whether governments should set minimum prices for agricultural products such as rice. [12]
A 12-mark discuss question needs two-sided analysis and a supported judgement. Plan it in note form — four or five bullet points — then compare with the model plan. Use Chain B from activity 4 and Part B of the Diagram Lab. Thailand’s 2011–14 rice scheme is ideal evidence.
Your plan
Model plan.
1 · Case for. Rice supply depends on the monsoon, and demand is price inelastic, so a good harvest causes a large fall in price. Farmers’ incomes are therefore unpredictable. A minimum price set above the equilibrium protects incomes, encourages farmers to keep planting and makes future food supplies more secure, with less need to buy on the world market (coursebook 12.3). Many low-income rural households depend on rice, so there is also an equity argument (Key Concept: equality and equity).
2 · Case against. Consumers pay a higher price for a staple food, which hits low-income urban households hardest. At the higher price, quantity demanded falls while farmers extend supply, so there is a surplus that the government must buy and store — a large cost with an opportunity cost. Resources stay in rice that could be used elsewhere: an inefficient allocation. Evidence: in 2011–14 Thailand paid farmers well above the market price; huge stockpiles built up, the scheme made very large losses, and Thailand lost its place as the world’s largest rice exporter.
3 · It depends on. How far above equilibrium the floor is set; how price elastic demand and supply are; whether the government can afford to buy the surplus; whether the problem is unstable incomes (a floor helps) or persistently low incomes (direct support may target better); short run versus long run.
4 · Judgement. A minimum price is justified as a safety net set only slightly above the long-run average price, because it stabilises incomes and food supply at a manageable cost. A high floor held for years, as in 2011–14, costs more than it achieves; direct income support for poor farmers meets the equity aim without creating a surplus.
Why this scores: both sides are analysed as chains, not lists; the evidence is specific; the “it depends” points lead straight into the judgement, which says when a minimum price is worth it.
