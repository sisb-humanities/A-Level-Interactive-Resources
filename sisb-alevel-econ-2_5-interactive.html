Activity 1 · Key terms

Match each term to its meaning

Topic 2.5 measures who gains from a market. Consumer surplus is the gain to buyers, producer surplus is the gain to sellers, and together they are the net benefit to society. Every diagram in this topic is about shading the right area — and saying what happens to it when something changes.

Work through activities 1–9 after the lessons, then activity 10 as a timed test. Everything here follows Chapter 11 of the coursebook (sections 11.1 and 11.2). Activities 2 and 6 build the ideas from real buyers and sellers; activities 3–5 are the diagrams; 7–10 are exam skills.

Activity 2 · Where surplus comes from

Eight hungry customers, eight stalls, one price

It is Friday at a Bangkok night market. Eight customers each want one portion of mango sticky rice, and each has a maximum they would pay. Eight stalls can each make one more portion, and each has a minimum they would accept — the extra cost of making it.

Everyone pays the same market price. Drag the orange price line (or use the slider). A customer’s consumer surplus is what they would have paid minus what they do pay; a stall’s producer surplus is what it receives minus its minimum. Put the bars side by side and you have built a demand curve and a supply curve — exactly the idea in section 11.1.

Activity 4 · Causes of changes in surplus

Predict it, watch the curve move, then measure the change

Six markets: the coursebook’s airline example and five from Thailand. For each one, make three predictions and lock them in. Then watch it in four stages: starting surplus → the shift → the new equilibrium → measuring the change.

The rule for stage 4: measure the change in surplus along the curve that did not move. When supply shifts, the demand curve still shows what consumers are willing to pay, so you can measure the change in consumer surplus exactly. When demand shifts, measure the change in producer surplus along the unchanged supply curve. This is how coursebook Figures 11.3 and 11.6 are drawn.

Check this against the coursebook

Section 11.1 and Figure 11.4 say that for the same price increase, the loss of consumer surplus is bigger when demand is elastic. Test that with the slider. When both curves start from the same price and quantity, the dollar loss is bigger when demand is inelastic: consumers keep buying, so they pay the higher price on almost every unit. The elastic case only loses more as a percentage of the consumer surplus it started with, because an elastic demand curve has a small triangle to begin with. The two diagrams in Figure 11.4 are drawn at different quantities, which is why they appear to show the opposite.

For producer surplus, the coursebook is right that a more elastic supply gains more from a price rise — in dollars and in percentage terms. (The printed text in section 11.2 says this is so “when demand is elastic”; it means supply.) In an exam, state which measure you are using: “in absolute terms…” or “as a proportion of the original surplus…”. That precision is itself an evaluation mark.

8
With the help of a diagram, explain how an increase in demand for flights from Bangkok to Chiang Mai during Songkran affects producer surplus, and why the size of the change depends on the price elasticity of supply. [8]
Plan before you write. Define producer surplus, shift demand, find the new equilibrium, then measure the change in producer surplus along the unchanged supply curve: a rectangle plus a triangle. Finish with PES. Activity 4 (the PIA flight) and activity 5 (producer surplus mode) are the models to copy.
Your answer
Model answer. Producer surplus is the difference between the price a producer is willing to accept for a unit and the price it actually receives; on a diagram it is the area above the supply curve and below the price. Before Songkran, many people want to travel home to Chiang Mai at the same time, so the demand for seats rises at every fare and the demand curve shifts right from D to D₁. At the original fare P there is excess demand, so airlines raise the fare to P₁ and there is an extension of supply from Q to Q₁ as extra flights are added. Producer surplus rises from the area between P and the supply curve to the larger area between P₁ and the supply curve. The gain has two parts: a rectangle (P₁ − P) × Q, because every seat that was already being sold now earns more above the airline’s minimum acceptable price, and a triangle for the extra seats Q to Q₁, each sold above its minimum price. The size of the gain depends on the price elasticity of supply. Over Songkran, aircraft, crews and airport slots are largely fixed, so supply is price inelastic: most of the effect is a large rise in fare with few extra seats, and the gain comes mainly from the rectangle. If airlines can add flights easily, supply is more elastic, the fare rises less, more seats are sold and the triangle is larger. For a given rise in price, a more elastic supply gives a larger gain in producer surplus.
Why this scores: it defines the term, shows the shift and the process (excess demand → higher fare → extension of supply), separates the rectangle from the triangle, and explains how PES changes the size of the effect rather than just saying it does.

12
Discuss whether online shopping festivals, such as the 11.11 sales on Shopee and Lazada, increase consumer surplus at the expense of producers. [12]
This is the coursebook’s ‘Think like an economist’ case on China’s Singles Day, brought to Thailand. A 12-mark discuss question needs two-sided analysis and a supported judgement. Plan it in note form — four or five bullet points — then compare with the model plan. Use what you learned in activities 4 and 6.
Your plan
Model plan.
1 · Case for. A one-day discount lowers the price. Consumers who would have bought anyway now pay less on every unit: that rectangle of surplus is transferred from producers to consumers. New buyers drawn in by the low price gain a triangle of consumer surplus as well. On units that would have sold at full price, sellers’ producer surplus clearly falls.
2 · Case against. Sellers choose to take part, so they expect to gain. Extra units sold add a triangle of producer surplus, which can outweigh the rectangle given away if demand is price elastic. A sale is also a form of price discrimination (activity 6): price-sensitive shoppers wait for 11.11 while others pay full price the rest of the year, so sellers convert surplus they could not otherwise capture. Sales clear old stock that would earn nothing later. Some “original” prices are raised before the sale, so the real discount — and the gain to consumers — is smaller than it looks. Impulse buying may mean the measured consumer surplus overstates real benefit if buyers regret purchases.
3 · It depends on. The price elasticity of demand for the products; whether 11.11 creates new spending or just moves spending from other months; the cost of the extra units (PES and capacity in delivery); and who the producers are — the platform earns commission on every sale, while small sellers may be pressured into discounts that hurt them.
4 · Judgement. Consumers usually gain, but mostly not “at the expense of producers” overall: much of the gain comes from extra trade that creates new surplus for both sides. The clearest losers are small sellers with thin margins who feel they must discount to stay visible, while the platform gains in all cases. So the claim is true for some producers, not producers as a whole.
Evaluation sentence starters: “This depends on how price elastic demand is…”, “The rectangle is a transfer, but the triangles are new surplus, so…”, “This assumes sellers did not raise prices before the sale…”, “On balance, consumers gain, but the cost falls mainly on…”
