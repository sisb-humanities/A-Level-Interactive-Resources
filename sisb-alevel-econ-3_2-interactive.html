Economics in context · India taxes vegetable oil imports
🛢️
India plans an extra 5% tax on vegetable oil imports, using the revenue for a fund that helps its own farmers grow more oilseed.
Demand for vegetable oil in India has trebled in 20 years; home output has risen by less than a third, so imports now cost close to $10 bn a year. Discuss with a partner, jot your ideas, then compare.
1 What micro and macro issues lie behind the plan? 2 If it works, who wins and who loses? 3 What could make it fail?
1 · Issues. Micro: the tax is ad valorem (5% of the price), so it shifts the supply of imported oil up and raises the price in India. Cooking oil is a staple with price-inelastic demand, so most of the tax is likely to be passed on to consumers. Macro: a large import bill worsens the current account, and relying on a few foreign suppliers is a food-security risk.
2 · Winners and losers. Winners: Indian oilseed farmers (higher demand for a close substitute, plus help from the fund) and the government (revenue). Losers: Indian households, especially low-income ones, who pay more for a necessity; food businesses; exporters in Indonesia, Malaysia, Latin America and the Black Sea region.
3 · Why it might fail. Oilseed supply is price inelastic in the short run — land, seeds and yields take years to change. Demand keeps rising with population and incomes. The fund could be poorly targeted or misused, and supplier countries could retaliate. Meanwhile consumers pay more, adding to inflation.

Keep the two kinds of indirect tax apart: a specific tax is a fixed amount per unit (฿ per litre), an ad valorem tax is a percentage of the price (Thailand’s 7% VAT, India’s 5% import tax). The Tax & Subsidy Lab shows why they shift supply differently.

8
With the help of a diagram, explain how the price elasticity of demand for a product affects the incidence of a specific indirect tax. [8]
Plan before you write. Define a specific tax and incidence, show the shift of supply, then compare an inelastic case (fuel) with an elastic case. Use the Tax & Subsidy Lab presets for Figures 13.3a and 13.3b as your two diagrams.
Your answer
Model answer. A specific tax is a fixed amount charged on each unit sold, such as ฿5 a litre on diesel. The incidence of the tax is how its burden is shared between consumers and producers. Because the seller must pay the tax to the government, the supply curve shifts vertically upwards by the amount of the tax, from S to S1, and the two curves are parallel. The market price rises from P to P1, but by less than the tax; the producer keeps only P2, which is P1 minus the tax. Consumers bear P1 − P per unit and producers bear P − P2. How the burden is split depends on price elasticity of demand. When demand is price inelastic, as for diesel, buyers have few substitutes and keep buying as the price rises, so sellers can pass most of the tax on: P1 rises almost by the full tax, quantity falls only a little and consumers bear most of the burden (Figure 13.3a). When demand is price elastic, as for one brand of bubble tea, consumers switch away when the price rises, so sellers cannot raise the price much without losing sales; they absorb most of the tax and the quantity falls sharply (Figure 13.3b). This explains why governments tax goods with inelastic demand, such as fuel and tobacco: the quantity falls little, so tax revenue is large and fairly predictable.
Why this scores: both terms are defined; the diagram is described in the text (S, S1, P, P1, P2); the shares are identified precisely; the explanation says why elasticity matters (substitutes, switching), not just that it does; it compares two contrasting cases and ends with an applied point about revenue.

12
Discuss whether a subsidy is a better way than a maximum price for a government to keep an essential good, such as diesel or cooking oil, affordable. [12]
A 12-mark discuss question needs two-sided analysis and a supported judgement. Plan in note form — four or five bullet points — then compare with the model plan. The Price Control Lab’s “clear the shortage with a subsidy” button is the diagram for this question.
Your plan
Model plan.
1 · Maximum price. Set below equilibrium, it lowers the price at no direct cost to the budget, so buyers who obtain the good gain. But quantity demanded extends while quantity supplied contracts: a shortage (Figure 13.7). The good must be allocated by queues or rationing, and an informal market develops at prices above the ceiling, so many low-income households may not benefit at all.
2 · Subsidy. Paid to producers, it lowers their costs and shifts supply to the right, so the price falls and the quantity rises — there is no shortage. Set at the right size, it can deliver the same low price as a ceiling while clearing the market (the combination in coursebook MCQ 1). The benefit is shared between consumers and producers.
3 · Costs of the subsidy. It is paid from tax revenue, so it has an opportunity cost, and it is a “blanket” payment: rich households that use more diesel or oil gain more in total. It removes the incentive for inefficient producers to cut costs. Evidence: Thailand’s Oil Fuel Fund held diesel prices down in 2022 and ran up a deficit of more than ฿100 bn.
4 · It depends on. The government’s budget and its other spending priorities; the price elasticities (the more elastic demand is, the more of a subsidy goes to producers); whether the price rise is temporary or permanent; whether low-income households can be targeted directly instead.
5 · Judgement. A subsidy is better than a maximum price for a temporary price shock to an essential good, because it keeps supply flowing and avoids shortages and informal markets. For a permanent rise it becomes a large, poorly targeted drain on the budget, and a targeted cash transfer to low-income households is likely to be better than either.
Why this scores: each method is analysed with its diagram; the costs are specific (shortage, informal market, opportunity cost, poor targeting); the evidence is real; the judgement says when each method wins rather than sitting on the fence.
