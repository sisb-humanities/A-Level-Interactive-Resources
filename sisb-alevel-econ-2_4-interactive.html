Activity 5 · Relationships between markets

A change in one market ripples into another

Section 10.3 of the coursebook links markets in four ways: alternative demand (substitutes), joint demand (complements), derived demand and joint supply. Each card below shows market A and market B side by side, following Figures 10.11–10.14.

Read the cause, predict what happens in market B, then press play. The key skill: in market B, work out which curve moves. It is not always demand.

A quick test for joint supply versus derived demand: in joint supply the two goods come out of the same production process (sugar and molasses). In derived demand one is an input needed to make the other (hotel workers for hotel stays).

8
With the help of diagrams, explain how an increase in the number of tourists visiting Phuket is likely to affect the market for hotel workers. [8]
Plan before you write. You need two diagrams: the market for hotel stays and the market for hotel workers. Name the relationship, then take each market from shift to new equilibrium. Activity 5, card 3, is the model to copy.
Your answer
Model answer. The demand for hotel workers is a derived demand: hotels employ cleaners, receptionists and chefs only because tourists want hotel stays. If more tourists visit Phuket, for example because new direct flights open, the demand for hotel stays increases and the demand curve shifts right from D to D₁. At the original price there is excess demand for rooms, so the price of a room-night rises and more room-nights are sold. To provide those extra room-nights, hotels need more workers at every wage, so the demand curve for hotel workers also shifts right from D to D₁. At the original wage W there is now excess demand for labour: hotels cannot fill their vacancies. The wage therefore rises to W₁. As it rises there is an extension of the supply of labour, a movement along the supply curve, as higher pay attracts people into hotel work from other jobs and other provinces. Employment rises from Q to Q₁. How much of the effect falls on wages rather than employment depends on how easily extra workers can be attracted: the more elastic the supply of labour, the smaller the rise in the wage.
Why this scores: it defines derived demand and explains the link, uses two linked diagrams, takes each market through excess demand to a new equilibrium, separates the shift in labour demand from the movement along labour supply, and ends with a qualification. Two complete chains, not two assertions.

12
Discuss whether the price mechanism can be relied upon to allocate resources effectively in agricultural markets such as coffee or durian. [12]
A 12-mark discuss question needs two-sided analysis and a supported judgement. Plan it here in note form — four or five bullet points is enough — then compare with the model plan. Use the coffee case from ‘Think like an economist’ (Figure 10.15) and what you saw on the slow setting in activity 2.
Your plan
Model plan.
1 · Case for. When Chinese demand for Thai durian rises, demand shifts right and price rises. The higher price rations the limited fruit to the buyers who value it most, signals to growers that consumers want more, and gives them an incentive to plant more trees. No government needs to know what consumers want: preferences are transmitted automatically. Kenyan passion fruit shows farmers switching crops in response.
2 · Case against. Agricultural supply responds slowly. A new tree crop takes years to bear fruit, so supply is very price inelastic in the short run and the market can stay in disequilibrium for a long time, with prices overshooting. Weather shifts supply unpredictably, so prices are volatile: Figure 10.15 shows the world coffee price more than halving between 2011 and 2019. Small farmers are price takers on a global market, so the outcome may be efficient but leaves many growers in poverty — the allocation is not necessarily fair.
3 · It depends on. The time period (short run versus long run); how quickly land can switch (passion fruit gives a first crop in its first season, durian and coffee take years); whether the product can be stored; and whether farmers have the information and credit to respond to the signal.
4 · Judgement. The price mechanism moves resources in the right direction, but in tree-crop markets it does so slowly and with large swings in price and income. It can be relied on more for quick-response crops than for coffee or durian, which is why buffer stocks, fair-trade agreements and diversification schemes exist — the case for intervention you will meet in topic 3.2.
Evaluation sentence starters: “This depends on how quickly…”, “In the short run… but in the long run…”, “This argument assumes farmers can respond, which may not hold because…”, “On balance, the stronger argument is… because…”
